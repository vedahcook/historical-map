How this map was made

Each year shows the borders in force on July 1. The map uses OpenHistoricalMap (OHM), which matched the historical record in 149 of 160 spot checks. Where OHM has no record, it falls back to CShapes-Europe (from 1816) or Cliopatria. Where our checks found OHM wrong, the map applies a correction and says so. Before 1800 OHM has fewer records: it often has the Holy Roman Empire without its member states, so many small German states show as the Empire, and Cliopatria fills more of the map. The maps for 1000–1499 and 1500–1799 load separately, a moment after the page opens.

Before 1500 OHM draws only part of Europe, and some of its records change only every few decades, so Cliopatria draws most of the map; its outlines change in steps of a few years and are rough at the coasts, so the medieval map is approximate in its details. France’s great fiefs, such as Normandy, Burgundy and Toulouse, are shown in their own colors, striped in France’s color, since they owed homage to the French king; lands in France held by the kings of England are in England’s color, striped in France’s. The larger members of the Holy Roman Empire show as their own states. 324 checks of places and years against the historical record all match, 17 of them where either of two answers is fair (for example Toledo in 1100, when Alfonso VI was king of both León and Castile).

Occupations: OHM records named occupation regimes, such as the General Government in Poland or the Allied zones of Germany, and the map uses them. Where OHM shows the country that held the land in law, the map adds occupations from Cliopatria’s year-by-year outlines of each power’s control (1914-46), land annexed during the Second World War and given back afterward, and the breakaway regions and occupied parts of Ukraine since 1990. Each of these is listed under corrections.

Alternative borders compare OHM with CShapes-Europe from 1816 and with Cliopatria before that. They count only when a source puts an area of at least 400 km² (5,000 km² for the coarser Cliopatria) in a different country for longer than a year. Territories that governed themselves under an overlord count as part of the overlord for this comparison, because the sources disagree on whether to show them.

Cities appear when a country is highlighted: the ten largest of its cities on screen that year, so zooming in brings in smaller towns. They come from about 4,900 cities and towns (Europe's largest by 1900, those that grew large after, large towns of 1000–1800, and every town that reached about 20,000 people), placed in whichever country held them that year. Tap one for its population. Figures come from censuses where there is one for that year; between figures the map estimates, assuming steady growth, and says so. A hollow dot means there is no figure within ten years (25 years before 1800, 50 before 1500); the map then gives the nearest figures instead of an estimate. The smaller towns’ figures come from Wikidata and from population tables in Wikipedia articles (checked against Wikidata), so they are thinner in Britain, Portugal, Turkey and before the mid-1800s, and zoomed-in views there can show fewer than ten. Before 1800 the figures are mostly Jan de Vries’s estimates every 50 years for towns that reached 10,000 people, which cover western and central Europe but not the Ottoman lands or Russia. Before 1500 there are figures for only about 60 cities, mostly Tertius Chandler’s estimates, so many countries show few or none. Early figures are estimates, some for the wider city, so they are not always comparable with later census counts.

Events and people come from Wikidata: battles, sieges and bombings; massacres, assassinations and attacks; uprisings, revolutions and protests; treaties, congresses and conferences; church councils and papal elections; coronations; great fires; earthquakes, floods and epidemics; world’s fairs and Olympic Games; and trials, crises, scandals and declarations. A city’s popup lists its most written-about events from 1000 on, up to 30 for a large city and 12 for a town, no more than about a third of them from any one century, and the people Wikidata records as born or died there who have articles in at least ten languages (up to 14 for a large city, 6 for a town). Wikidata sometimes records an event or a birth for a district or region rather than the city, so some are missing.

Letting go of the year slider zooms the timeline in around that year: from a few years to a few centuries, as many as it takes to hold about ten of the events on screen (or in the highlighted country), which are then listed below it. The arrows at its ends, or dragging it sideways, move along. The thin bar above it shows all the years, with the stretch on view marked: drag along it to move quickly through time (the timeline zooms in again where you let go), or press “All years” to zoom out. Borders still change once a year, on July 1.

Terrain: the shaded relief is drawn from NASA’s elevation data (about 2 km to the pixel) and lit from the northwest; rivers and lakes come from Natural Earth and add detail as you zoom in. Man-made reservoirs show only from the year they were filled (the Kakhovka Reservoir until its dam was destroyed in 2023). Names of physical features (seas and straits, lakes and lagoons, mountain ranges, plains, rivers, and passes and peaks that mattered in Europe’s history) are a layer of their own, set along each feature in a serif italic, and appear as the zoom allows. A few take the name of the time: the IJsselmeer is the Zuiderzee until it was dammed in 1932. The mountain button beside the zoom buttons turns the relief and these names on and off.
